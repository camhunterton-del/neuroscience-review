Resources

What we recommend.

The books, conferences, podcasts, and journals we point to when a reader wants to go past the article and into the field itself.

Everything here is something a curious, careful reader can actually use. Nothing made the list because it is popular. It is here because it is accurate, well made, and still active. Every link below goes to the publisher or the official site.

Jump to Books · Conferences · Podcasts · Reading the research · Courses · Organizations

Books

Eight we keep coming back to. A mix of accessible popular science and a couple of foundational works for going all the way.

Being You by Anil Seth. A working neuroscientist's case that what you experience is the brain's best guess about the world, not a direct readout of it. Accessible without cutting corners.

The Idea of the Brain by Matthew Cobb. A history of how each era pictured the brain in terms of its newest technology, and how often that led thinking astray. Clear about what we still cannot explain.

Behave by Robert Sapolsky. Follows a single human action outward from the neurons that fired a second before it to the evolution behind it. Long, rigorous, and genuinely funny.

The Brain That Changes Itself by Norman Doidge. The book that brought neuroplasticity to general readers, built around case stories of brains rewiring after injury and loss.

Livewired by David Eagleman. A current account of how the brain constantly reconfigures itself around the inputs it receives, drawing on Eagleman's own lab.

Phantoms in the Brain by V. S. Ramachandran and Sandra Blakeslee. A classic that uses strange cases, phantom limbs and hemineglect among them, to show how the ordinary brain is put together.

The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat by Oliver Sacks. The classic collection of clinical tales, still the high-water mark for writing about the brain with humanity.

Principles of Neural Science edited by Eric Kandel and colleagues. The field's foundational reference textbook, assembled largely at Columbia. Where to turn when you want the full, formal treatment.

Conferences

The meetings where neuroscience is argued out in person. Dates shift from year to year, so each name links to the official site for the current schedule.

Society for Neuroscience (SfN) Annual Meeting. The largest neuroscience gathering in the world, spanning every subfield from molecules to behavior. Recurs annually each fall, in a rotating US city.

Cognitive Neuroscience Society (CNS) Annual Meeting. Focused on the brain basis of the mind, from attention and memory to language. Recurs annually each spring.

FENS Forum, from the Federation of European Neuroscience Societies. Europe's largest neuroscience meeting, running from basic research to the clinic. Recurs every two years, in even-numbered years, in a rotating European city.

COSYNE, Computational and Systems Neuroscience. A single-track meeting where experimental and theoretical approaches to how neural circuits compute meet. Recurs annually in late winter, alternating between North America and Europe.

Organization for Human Brain Mapping (OHBM) Annual Meeting. The main meeting for human brain imaging, covering fMRI, EEG, and related methods. Recurs annually each June, rotating among continents.

Gordon Research Conferences in Neuroscience. Small, intensive, off-the-record meetings on tightly focused topics, each with its own subject and chair. Most run over the summer.

Podcasts

A few shows worth following, each still releasing new episodes.

Brain Inspired by Paul Middlebrooks. Long-form interviews at the border of neuroscience and artificial intelligence, for people who want the real arguments rather than a summary.

Many Minds from the Diverse Intelligences Summer Institute, hosted by Kensy Cooperrider. Conversations on how minds work across humans, other animals, and machines.

Huberman Lab by Andrew Huberman. The most widely heard neuroscience podcast, where a Stanford neurobiologist translates brain and body science into practical takeaways. Strongest on the underlying science, and worth weighing the day-to-day protocols for yourself.

Reading the research

Where the primary neuroscience actually lives. Most papers sit behind a paywall, but abstracts are free to read, and open-access options keep growing.

Nature Neuroscience. One of the top venues for primary neuroscience research, from molecular to cognitive work.

Neuron. A leading journal across molecular, cellular, systems, and cognitive neuroscience, published by Cell Press.

The Journal of Neuroscience. The Society for Neuroscience's own journal, broad in scope and published weekly.

eLife. Open access, and now publishing reviewed preprints with the public peer reviews attached, so you can read the critique alongside the paper.

Nature Communications. A large open-access journal with a deep neuroscience section, much of it free to read.

bioRxiv, neuroscience preprints. Where a great deal of neuroscience appears first. Read it for what is coming, keeping in mind that preprints have not yet been peer reviewed.

Courses and lectures

Ways to learn the field itself, from full university courses to open teaching resources you can work through at your own pace. Where the material is free to audit or openly posted, we say so. Enrollment windows shift, so each name links to the official page.

Medical Neuroscience from Duke University, taught by Leonard White. The same course Duke gives first-year medical students, rebuilt for anyone, running from the single neuron up through the sensory and motor systems to higher function. Demanding and thorough, and the one to pick if you want the full clinical-grade treatment. Free to audit on Coursera, with an optional paid certificate.

The Human Brain, MIT OpenCourseWare 9.13, taught by Nancy Kanwisher. A full undergraduate cognitive neuroscience course with the lecture videos, notes, and assignments posted free. Especially strong on how imaging and other methods actually let us read the brain, and on what they cannot tell us.

Fundamentals of Neuroscience from HarvardX on edX, created by David Cox. A three-part series that builds up from the electrical behavior of one neuron to whole circuits and the brain, with interactive simulations the whole way. Free to audit, and a good fit for a motivated beginner who likes learning by building.

HHMI BioInteractive. Free, classroom-tested videos, animations, and virtual labs from the Howard Hughes Medical Institute, with a solid run of neuroscience material on neurons, action potentials, and the nervous system. Pitched at high school and early college, and a gentle on-ramp when the primary research feels steep.

Neuromatch Academy. An online school for computational neuroscience whose full curriculum, from modeling and machine learning to the dynamics of neural circuits, is posted free and openly licensed. Best for readers with a little Python who want to see how the field actually models the brain.

Allen Brain Map, from the Allen Institute. The open data platform behind a great deal of modern neuroscience, with brain atlases, cell-type maps, and free lesson plans and tutorials built on the same datasets its own scientists use. For the hands-on reader who wants to explore real data, not just read about it.

Organizations

The institutions that fund the research, run the meetings, and put a surprising amount out for the public. Each one is worth knowing, and each gives a curious reader something to use directly.

Society for Neuroscience (SfN). The largest neuroscience organization in the world and the body behind the field's biggest annual meeting and its flagship journals. Its free public arm, BrainFacts.org, is one of the best plain-language introductions to the brain anywhere.

Allen Institute for Brain Science. A Seattle research institute that maps the brain's cells and wiring at enormous scale and gives every dataset away free. If you have ever wanted to open a real brain atlas and look around, this is where it lives.

Kavli Foundation. A philanthropy that backs basic science, with neuroscience among its main pillars and a network of Kavli neuroscience institutes around the world. A good window into where the field is being pushed next.

Dana Foundation. A foundation focused on neuroscience and society and a longtime champion of public brain education. Its resources explain what the science means for everyday life, and it helped launch Brain Awareness Week.

Federation of European Neuroscience Societies (FENS). The umbrella body for European neuroscience, representing more than 20,000 researchers and running the FENS Forum. A useful vantage point on how the field looks beyond the United States.

Simons Foundation. A large private funder of mathematics and basic science whose brain work runs from autism research through SFARI to the computational neuroscience at its Flatiron Institute. Much of what it funds ends up as openly shared tools and findings.
