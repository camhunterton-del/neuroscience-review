Depression probably isn't a serotonin shortage

The chemical imbalance story was simple, hopeful, and almost certainly wrong as a cause of depression. Here is what the evidence actually says, why the drugs can still help anyway, and why the framing matters more than it looks.

July 15, 2026 · 6 min read · Cameron Hunterton
Key takeaways
  • The chemical imbalance idea, that depression comes from too little serotonin, was an inference from how early drugs behaved, not a proven cause. A 2022 umbrella review pulling together decades of evidence found no consistent sign that depressed people have lower serotonin.
  • Antidepressants do beat placebo across very large trials, but the average benefit is modest, and a drug helping does not prove the illness is a shortage of whatever the drug touches.
  • Depression is real and often treatable. The honest picture is a whole-person problem we do not fully understand, not one broken chemical. Never stop a prescribed antidepressant because of an article. That is a conversation for a doctor.

The story goes like this. Depression is caused by a chemical imbalance in the brain. You are low on serotonin, the feel-good chemical, and antidepressants like Prozac work by topping it back up. Get serotonin back to normal and the depression lifts, the same way insulin fixes a sugar problem in diabetes.

Most people have heard some version of this. For years it was printed in patient leaflets and repeated in drug ads. It is simple, it is hopeful, and it is almost certainly wrong, at least as a description of what causes depression. Here is what the evidence actually says, and why the framing matters more than it looks.

Where the idea came from

The serotonin story is a narrowed-down version of an older one. In the 1960s, researchers noticed that drugs that happened to raise brain chemicals called monoamines, a family of neurotransmitters that includes serotonin, noradrenaline, and dopamine, seemed to lift mood in some patients. Drugs that lowered those same chemicals sometimes did the opposite. From that came the monoamine hypothesis, the idea that low monoamines cause depression.

It was a reasonable guess for its time. It was never a proven mechanism. It was an inference working backwards from what the drugs seemed to do. When SSRIs arrived in the late 1980s, drugs that raise serotonin in particular, the marketing sanded the hypothesis down into a slogan. Low serotonin. Chemical imbalance. Take this to fix it.

What the evidence actually shows

In 2022, a team led by Joanna Moncrieff published a systematic umbrella review in the journal Molecular Psychiatry. An umbrella review is a study of studies of studies, a way of pulling together the strongest existing evidence in one place. They looked at every major line of research that should turn up a serotonin difference if the theory were true. Serotonin and its breakdown products in body fluids. Serotonin receptor levels. The serotonin transporter. Experiments that artificially lower serotonin. Genetics.

Across all of it, they found no consistent evidence that depressed people have lower serotonin than anyone else. Not in the blood, not in the brain imaging, and not in the receptor studies once you account for things like past medication use. The review was contested, and critics argued it brushed past some nuances. But even its opponents mostly agreed on the core point. There is no solid evidence that depression is caused by a simple lack of serotonin.

The cleanest clue

One line of research makes the point especially well. Scientists can briefly lower serotonin in a living person by having them drink a mix of amino acids that crowds out the raw material the brain uses to build it. If low serotonin caused depression, this should reliably sink people's mood.

It does not. A meta-analysis by Ruhé and colleagues in 2007 gathered these studies and found that lowering serotonin did not make healthy people depressed. It nudged mood a little only in people who already had a personal or family history of depression, which points to some underlying vulnerability, not to a serotonin shortage sitting at the root of the illness.

But the drugs work, don't they

Here is where people get understandably confused. If serotonin is not the cause, why do serotonin drugs help anyone at all? And they do help. A large network meta-analysis by Cipriani and colleagues in 2018, covering 522 trials and roughly 116,000 patients, found that every one of the 21 antidepressants tested beat placebo. That is a real effect.

But notice the size of it. The average benefit over placebo was modest. An earlier analysis by Kirsch in 2008, using the full set of trial data drug companies had submitted to regulators for several newer antidepressants, found the gap between drug and placebo was small for most patients and only clearly meaningful at the very severe end. Averages like that hide a lot of individual variation, and plenty of people below that line still get real relief from these drugs.

So the drugs do something. They are simply not the clean serotonin top-up the slogan promised. And a drug helping does not prove the disease is a shortage of whatever the drug touches. Aspirin eases a headache. Nobody thinks headaches are caused by a lack of aspirin. Antidepressants change brain chemistry in ways we still do not fully understand, and some of that change seems to help some people. That can be true at the same time that the cause is not a serotonin deficit.

Why the framing matters

This is not a word game. The story you are told about your own suffering changes how you carry it.

Being told that depression is a permanent chemical defect in your brain sounds scientific, even reassuring at first. But when researchers actually tested it, telling people their depression came from a chemical imbalance did not reduce their self-blame, and it left them more pessimistic about ever getting better and less confident they could manage their own moods. That is a heavy thing to hand someone based on a slogan the evidence never supported.

The honest version is both truer and, oddly, more hopeful. Depression is real. It is not imaginary and it is not a character flaw. But it looks less like a single broken chemical and more like a whole-person problem, tangled up in genes, stress, sleep, circumstance, and the brain's wiring, that we are still working to understand.

Aspirin eases a headache. Nobody thinks headaches are caused by a lack of aspirin.

What we can honestly say

If you want a short replacement for the wrong version, try this. Depression is real and often treatable. Serotonin is involved in mood somehow, but low serotonin is not a proven cause of depression, and the chemical imbalance story was always more marketing than science. Antidepressants help some people, modestly, for reasons we have not fully pinned down.

None of this means the drugs are fake or that anyone should stop taking them. If you are on an antidepressant and it helps you, that is real, and stopping suddenly can be genuinely dangerous. This is a conversation to have with a doctor, not a blog.

What the science asks of us is humility. We treat depression with tools that work better than nothing while being honest that we do not fully understand the thing we are treating. That is less tidy than a slogan. It is also the truth.

  2. Cipriani A, Furukawa TA, Salanti G, et al. “Comparative efficacy and acceptability of 21 antidepressant drugs for the acute treatment of adults with major depressive disorder.” The Lancet, 2018. (All 21 beat placebo, effect modest.)
  3. Kirsch I, Deacon BJ, Huedo-Medina TB, Scoboria A, Moore TJ, Johnson BT. “Initial severity and antidepressant benefits: a meta-analysis of data submitted to the Food and Drug Administration.” PLoS Medicine, 2008. (The drug-placebo gap and where it becomes meaningful.)
  4. Ruhé HG, Mason NS, Schene AH. “Mood is indirectly related to serotonin, norepinephrine and dopamine levels in humans: a meta-analysis of monoamine depletion studies.” Molecular Psychiatry, 2007. (Lowering serotonin does not depress healthy people.)
  5. Kemp JJ, Lickel JJ, Deacon BJ. “Effects of a chemical imbalance causal explanation on individuals' perceptions of their depressive symptoms.” Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2014. (What the chemical imbalance framing does to patients.)
  6. Schildkraut JJ. “The catecholamine hypothesis of affective disorders.” American Journal of Psychiatry, 1965. (Historical origin of the monoamine idea.)
