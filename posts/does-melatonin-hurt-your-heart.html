Does melatonin actually hurt your heart?

A study linking long-term melatonin use to a 90 percent higher rate of heart failure went around the internet last November, and then again at the end of August. It has still never been peer-reviewed, and one of its own numbers points at a simpler explanation.

September 9, 2026 · 10 min read · Cameron Hunterton · Fact-checked
Share X Bluesky LinkedIn Facebook Email
Key takeaways
  • A database study of 130,828 adults with insomnia reported that those on melatonin for a year or more had heart failure at 4.6 percent over five years versus 2.7 percent in matched non-users, a hazard ratio of 1.89. That is the “90 percent” in every headline.
  • It is a conference abstract, presented in November 2025 and, as of today, still not published in a peer-reviewed journal. The August 2026 wave of coverage was the same unreviewed study going around a second time.
  • The comparison is the weak point. Everyone in the study had insomnia, but nobody measured how badly. Taking a sleep drug nightly for a year is itself a marker of worse insomnia, and insomnia on its own already carries a higher risk of heart failure.
  • The internal numbers fit that reading. Only 4.6 percent of users were newly diagnosed with heart failure, yet 19.0 percent were hospitalized for it — figures that cannot share a denominator, and that point to an exposed group already carrying established heart disease.

If you took melatonin last night, you were doing something tens of millions of Americans do. It is sold in gas stations, in gummy form, in doses of five and ten milligrams, and it carries an aura of harmlessness that almost nothing else in the sleep aisle has. It is a hormone your own pineal gland makes. It is not a sedative. It does not build tolerance the way a benzodiazepine does. For a long time the honest scientific position on melatonin was that it works modestly for shifting a sleep schedule, works less well than people think for chronic insomnia, and appears to be safe.

Then, on August 29, a fresh wave of coverage said long-term users had a 90 percent higher risk of heart failure. If you follow health news closely, you may have felt a flicker of recognition, because the same finding, with the same number, went around in November. That is not a coincidence, and the reason it happened twice is most of the story.

What the study actually did

The work is an abstract by Ekenedilichukwu Nnadi and colleagues, presented at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions in November 2025 and published as a conference abstract in Circulation. It is a retrospective cohort study built on TriNetX, a research network that pools de-identified electronic health records from health systems in the US and elsewhere. Nobody was recruited, randomized, or followed prospectively. The researchers queried records that already existed.

They pulled adults carrying an insomnia diagnosis. The exposed group needed at least one melatonin prescription and at least 365 days of exposure. The comparison group had no melatonin exposure in the record. That produced 65,414 people on each side, 130,828 in total, and the two groups were matched on demographics, fifteen comorbidities, cardiometabolic medications, laboratory values, vital signs, and how much health care they used. Then the researchers counted events over five years.

The matching deserves credit. Fifteen comorbidities plus labs, vitals, and utilization is a serious attempt to make two groups comparable, and it rules out the laziest version of the objection, which is that melatonin users were simply older or sicker in some obvious, recorded way. The problem is not what the researchers matched on. It is what no electronic health record contains.

What it found

Over five years, incident heart failure occurred in 4.6 percent of melatonin users and 2.7 percent of non-users, a hazard ratio of 1.89 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.78 to 2.00. Heart failure hospitalization occurred in 19.0 percent versus 6.6 percent, a hazard ratio of 3.44. Death from any cause occurred in 7.8 percent versus 4.3 percent, a hazard ratio of 2.09.

Those confidence intervals are narrow, which is what 130,000 people buys you. Narrowness means the association is unlikely to be a fluke of sampling. It says nothing at all about whether the association is causal, and the two get confused constantly. A very precisely measured artifact is still an artifact.

The comparison is doing the work

Here is the thing that should stop you. Everyone in this study had insomnia. Both groups. The exposed group was not defined by being sicker on paper, it was defined by having taken a sleep drug every night for at least a year.

Think about who that is. People whose insomnia resolved in a month are not in the melatonin group. People who tried it, found it useless, and stopped are not in the melatonin group. What remains, after a year of continuous use, is enriched for people whose sleep problem was bad enough and persistent enough to keep medicating nightly for twelve months. The control group is everyone else with an insomnia code, including the mild, the transient, and the resolved.

Epidemiologists call this confounding by indication: the treatment marks the severity of the condition, and the condition is what drives the outcome. It is the single most common way an observational drug study goes wrong, and it is not a hypothetical concern here, because insomnia is an established cardiovascular risk factor in its own right. A meta-analysis of seven prospective cohorts covering 177,008 people found insomnia symptoms associated with incident heart failure at a hazard ratio of 1.26. Broader work, including Mendelian randomization studies that use genetic variants to approximate a natural experiment, supports insomnia being causally upstream of several cardiovascular diseases.

So the study is not really comparing melatonin against no melatonin. It is comparing severe, persistent, year-plus insomnia against everything else filed under the same diagnosis code, and severity was never measured. The abstract also lacks data on melatonin dose and on psychiatric comorbidity, which travels with both bad insomnia and worse cardiovascular outcomes.

There is a further wrinkle specific to the US. Melatonin is sold over the counter as a dietary supplement, so a prescription record captures a strange, non-representative slice of users, and the “non-users” are only people with no melatonin in the record. An unknown number of them were buying it at the pharmacy counter. Misclassification like that usually pushes an association toward zero rather than inflating it, so it does not manufacture the finding, but it does mean the two groups are not what their labels say.

The number that gives it away

The internal pattern is the strongest clue. Melatonin use was associated with heart failure diagnosis at 1.89, and with heart failure hospitalization at 3.44. Sit with that gap.

Start with the raw percentages, because they do not fit together. Only 4.6 percent of melatonin users picked up a new heart failure diagnosis, yet 19.0 percent were hospitalized for heart failure. Those two figures cannot be counting the same denominator. You cannot admit four times more people for a disease than the number who newly developed it, so the hospitalization figure has to be drawing on a wider pool, almost certainly one that includes people who already carried a heart failure diagnosis before the window opened. The abstract does not say which, and that omission matters more than anything else in it.

Follow that through. If the melatonin arm was carrying materially more established heart disease at baseline, then the matching did not equalize the variable that most determines the outcome, and the incident-diagnosis figure is standing on the same tilted ground as the hospitalization one. Sicker, more medically entangled people get admitted more. They also die more, which is the 2.09. That single reading accounts for all three numbers without melatonin doing anything to anyone's heart.

A drug that damaged hearts should not be nearly twice as good at predicting hospital admission as it is at predicting the disease itself.

None of this proves melatonin is safe. It means this particular study cannot tell you either way, and its own numbers hint at the reason.

Why you saw it twice

Abstracts presented at scientific meetings are screened for relevance, not refereed. They are typically a few hundred words, carry no methods section a reader can interrogate, and are explicitly labeled preliminary until a full manuscript clears peer review. The American Heart Association says so plainly in its own release.

This one was presented in November 2025 and covered heavily. As of today, roughly ten months later, I can find no peer-reviewed publication of it. Then, at the end of August 2026, the finding recirculated as though it were fresh, and a second round of coverage carried the same 90 percent figure to a new audience. Nothing about the evidence changed in between. What changed was that it got written up again.

That is worth naming because it is a general failure mode, not a melatonin problem. A preliminary result enters the record, gets amplified, and its provisional status quietly falls away with each retelling. Ten months on, the honest description is unchanged: an unreviewed conference abstract with a plausible alternative explanation.

What is actually worth worrying about

The irony is that melatonin has real problems, and they are not this one.

The doses people take are pharmacological, not physiological. The amount that raises your blood melatonin to normal nighttime levels is roughly 0.3 milligrams. The bottles on the shelf commonly hold five or ten, which is ten to thirty times that.

And the labels are unreliable, because melatonin is regulated as a supplement rather than a drug. A 2017 analysis in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine found that more than 71 percent of products fell outside a 10 percent margin of their label claim, ranging from 83 percent below to 478 percent above, with lot-to-lot variation within a single product as high as 465 percent. Some products also contained serotonin, which has no business being in a sleep supplement. Later work on gummies marketed for children found doses spanning from none of the labeled amount to several times it.

There is also evidence pointing the other way on the heart specifically. Melatonin is an antioxidant, and a body of animal and early clinical work has explored it as potentially protective in cardiac ischemia and heart failure. That literature is not settled either, and animal cardioprotection does not transfer to humans on its own. But it means the biological prior for melatonin as a cardiotoxin is weak, which is one more reason to treat a single unreviewed database association carefully.

The bottom line

If you take melatonin occasionally to shift your sleep schedule, this study gives you no reason to stop. If you have been taking it every night for years, the useful question it raises is not “is melatonin poisoning me” but “why am I still needing a sleep aid every night after a year, and has anyone actually treated the insomnia?” Chronic insomnia has a first-line treatment with good evidence behind it, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, and it is not a supplement. Persistent insomnia is a reason to see a clinician on its own merits, independent of anything in this abstract.

What would settle the question is a study that compares people matched on insomnia severity, that knows the dose, and that has cleared peer review. That study does not exist yet. Until it does, the finding is a hypothesis with a large sample size attached, and a large sample size is not the same thing as an answer.

  1. Nnadi E, et al. “Abstract 4371606: Effect of Long-term Melatonin Supplementation on Incidence of Heart Failure in Patients with Insomnia.” Circulation, 2025. (The anchor. A conference abstract, not a peer-reviewed paper.)
  2. American Heart Association. “Long-term use of melatonin supplements to support sleep may have negative health effects.” Scientific Sessions 2025 news release. (The AHA's own statement of the numbers and the preliminary status.)
  3. Science Media Centre. “Expert reaction to conference abstract on melatonin supplementation and incidence of heart failure in patients with insomnia.” 2025. (Independent researchers on the limits of the design.)
  4. American College of Cardiology. “Research Suggests Long-Term Melatonin Use For Insomnia Increases HF Risk.” 2025. (The cohort definitions and matching variables.)
  5. Meta-analysis of insomnia and heart failure risk, seven prospective cohorts, 177,008 participants. Journal of Cardiology, 2025. journal-of-cardiology.com (Insomnia alone, hazard ratio 1.26.)
  6. “Associations between insomnia and cardiovascular diseases: a meta-review and meta-analysis of observational and Mendelian randomization studies.” 2024. (Evidence that insomnia is causally upstream of cardiovascular disease.)
  7. Erland LAE, Saxena PK. “Melatonin Natural Health Products and Supplements: Presence of Serotonin and Significant Variability of Melatonin Content.” Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 2017. (The 71 percent off-label figure, the 83-percent-under to 478-percent-over range, and the serotonin.)

  9. “Melatonin in Heart Failure: A Promising Therapeutic Strategy?” Molecules, 2018. (The literature pointing the opposite direction, largely preclinical.)
