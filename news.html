Recent brain research, each note checked against its source and linked to the original.

July 30, 2026 · via Medical Xpress / Molecular Psychiatry

Larger Choroid Plexus Size and Lower MRI Signal May Point to Future Dementia Risk

In a study of more than 45,000 UK Biobank participants, people with larger choroid plexus volume and lower T1 MRI signal intensity had higher rates of future dementia, especially Alzheimer's disease, along with faster cognitive decline.

This is one observational study showing a statistical link, not proof that these brain changes cause dementia.

July 30, 2026 · via EurekAlert! (American Society for Nutrition / NUTRITION 2026)

Small Trial Finds Time-Restricted Eating May Support Planning and Problem-Solving in Older Women

In a pilot study of 47 overweight or obese women aged 50 to 79, those who ate all their food within an 8 to 9 hour window each day showed modest improvements in planning and problem-solving compared with women who followed the same calorie deficit without a set eating window.

This is one small pilot trial with 47 participants, so it does not show broad or lasting cognitive benefits and needs confirmation in larger studies.

July 29, 2026 · via EurekAlert (TGen/University of Chicago)

SuperAgers Keep Sharp Memories Without Special Genetic Luck

A study of 142 SuperAgers and 89 typically aging older adults found their APOE gene status and Alzheimer's polygenic risk scores were statistically similar to average older adults. This suggests their exceptional memory is not simply the result of inheriting lower genetic risk.

The study only rules out this specific genetic explanation. It does not identify what actually protects SuperAgers' memory, and it has not yet been replicated in other groups.

July 28, 2026 · via Journal of Neuroscience

Study Finds Separate Brain Signals for Grasp Timing and Force

Researchers recorded sensorimotor cortex activity and found that the timing of an attempted grasp and the force behind it are carried by distinct neural patterns. The finding could help guide future brain computer interfaces aimed at restoring hand function.

The work is an offline decoding study and does not show a real time device that actually restores hand movement.

July 28, 2026 · via Medical Xpress

Heavy TV watching in midlife linked to smaller brain volumes years later

In a study of about 1,700 adults, those who said they watched television very often in midlife later had smaller brain regions tied to memory, executive function, and vision, along with more white matter damage. The link held even after accounting for physical activity and vascular risk factors.

This is one observational study using self reported TV habits, so it cannot prove that watching television causes these brain changes and other lifestyle factors could still play a role.

July 28, 2026 · via The Transmitter

Brain implants show motor cortex neurons respond to many body parts at once, not just one region

Researchers recorded single neurons from microelectrode arrays implanted in the precentral gyrus of 8 people with paralysis and found that many neurons responded to signals linked to more than one body part, rather than each neuron belonging to a single arm, leg, or face zone as the classic motor homunculus model suggested.

The study covered only 8 people with paralysis and sampled a limited patch of cortex per person, so it does not show whether this pattern holds in people without paralysis or in unsampled brain regions.

July 27, 2026 · via Phys.org

Silencing One Neuron Type Reverses Older Bees Back to Nursing Behavior

Researchers turned off doublesex expressing neurons in older honeybee brains and found the bees stopped foraging and went back to nursing tasks typical of much younger bees. This shows that one identified brain circuit can drive a switch in job type within a colony.

This comes from one study on one circuit in one set of colonies, so it does not show whether the effect lasts, applies to other bee lines, or accounts for the full system that assigns jobs in a hive.

July 23, 2026 · via Journal of Neuroscience

Study maps how the brain briefly pauses eye movements during ongoing visual exploration

Researchers tracked saccadic inhibition, the brief pause in eye movement that follows a visual event, while people viewed scenes continuously over time. They found this pausing response weakens with repeated exposure, showing the oculomotor system adapts the longer viewing continues.

This is one lab study using a specific continuous viewing task, so it does not show whether the same pattern holds during everyday natural viewing outside controlled conditions.

July 23, 2026 · via Mount Sinai Newsroom

Scientists Map Structure of Brain Protein Tied to Epilepsy and Autism, Create First Blocking Compound

Researchers at Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai obtained the first high resolution structure of the brain protein NBCn2, which is linked to epilepsy and autism spectrum disorder. They also created a new compound that blocks the protein's activity and reduced brain activity in tissue studies.

The compound was tested only in tissue samples, not in animals or people, so it does not yet show it can treat epilepsy or autism in a living organism.

July 22, 2026 · via Nature Neuroscience

Brain's Theta Sweeps Point Toward Remembered Goals Not Just Movement, Rat Study Finds

In rats running a maze built to separate head direction, movement direction, and goal direction, researchers found that brief hippocampal signals called theta sweeps pointed toward remembered goal locations regardless of which way the rat was actually moving or facing. These goal-direction signals grew stronger right before the rats made correct choices in the maze.

This shows a link between stronger theta sweep signals and correct choices in one maze task in rats, not proof that the signals cause the choices, and it has not been tested in humans or other navigation settings.

July 21, 2026 · via ScienceDaily

Brain Organoids From Alzheimer's Patients React Differently to an Antidepressant

Scientists grew miniature brain tissue models from Alzheimer's patients' cells and found the tissue responded differently to an antidepressant than tissue from non-Alzheimer's donors. The team says the organoids and the tiny particles they release could eventually help tailor treatments or aid diagnosis.

This is early lab based research on grown tissue models, not a clinical trial in people, so it does not yet prove any real treatment or diagnostic benefit.

July 21, 2026 · via Phys.org (Nature Communications)

Study suggests human brain growth and face shrinkage may reflect slower evolutionary drift rather than strong natural selection

Researchers from the University of Tennessee Knoxville and the University of Tübingen analyzed the pattern of brain enlargement and jaw reduction across human evolution and found the data fit a slower drift like process better than a model driven mainly by natural selection. The team did not conclude that selection played no role only that its influence may be smaller than the traditional story assumes.

This is one statistical analysis of evolutionary patterns not a direct test of what selective pressures existed in the past and it does not rule out natural selection entirely.

July 21, 2026 · via Nature

Scientists map how a fruit fly's brain and nerve cord work together to control behavior

Researchers built the first complete wiring map linking an adult fruit fly's brain to its ventral nerve cord, the insect equivalent of a spinal cord. The map shows that neurons controlling movement mostly rely on local sensory loops within the same body segment, which then connect through longer circuits tied to specific behaviors.

This is a structural map of neural connections in one fly, not a test of how the circuits actually function during live behavior.

July 18, 2026 · via USC Today

Dementia risk factors vary widely by country, large global study finds

A USC led study of more than 214,000 older adults across 14 countries found that the same dementia risk factors are far more common in some countries than in others. For example, low education affects 86% of older adults in China but only 12% in the United States, and high BMI is common in 45% of Americans but just 13% of Indians.

This is a single observational study showing differences in risk factor patterns across countries, it does not prove that changing these factors will reduce dementia cases or account for differences in how data was collected in each region.

July 17, 2026 · via Nature Neuroscience

AI Model Reads Typed Sentences From Brain Signals in a Lab Study

Researchers built a deep learning system called Brain2Qwerty that turned non-invasive EEG or MEG brain recordings into typed text for 35 healthy volunteers. The best participants reached a character error rate as low as 18 percent, and MEG far outperformed EEG.

This was a small lab demonstration using bulky MEG equipment, not tested with real time typing or in people with paralysis, and the 18 percent figure only reflects the top performers rather than typical results.

July 16, 2026 · via Nature Neuroscience

Mouse study finds brain circuit linking positive emotions to narcolepsy attacks

Researchers studying a mouse model of narcolepsy found that a specific oxytocin sensitive circuit in the amygdala controls cataplexy attacks triggered by social play and rewarding experiences. Turning this circuit on or off in mice directly caused or prevented these sudden muscle collapse episodes.

This work was done in mice and does not yet show whether the same circuit works the same way in people with narcolepsy.

July 15, 2026 · via ScienceDaily / Oregon State University

Sugar Coated Nanoparticles Extend Survival in Mice With Brain Cancer

Oregon State University researchers used sugar coated nanoparticles to carry genetic instructions that restore a tumor suppressing protein into brain cancer cells. In mice with this cancer median survival rose by 50 percent and tumors shrank without visible damage to other organs.

This is a single preliminary mouse study, so it does not show whether the treatment is safe or effective in humans.

July 15, 2026 · via MIT News (Picower Institute for Learning and Memory)

MIT study adds new evidence that local electric fields help guide brain cell activity

Researchers studying macaque monkeys during a working memory task found that moment to moment changes in neural firing lined up with local electric field patterns, supporting the idea of ephaptic coupling, a process where electric fields made by groups of neurons can influence nearby neurons. The findings were published in the journal Cerebral Cortex.

This is one study in animals using correlational and computational methods, so it does not prove electric fields are the main or only cause of these activity changes, and it has not yet been shown to apply to humans.

July 15, 2026 · via MIT News (McGovern Institute / York University)

Learning to Spot New Objects Slightly Reshapes Visual Brain Activity

Researchers from MIT and York University recorded brain activity in the inferior temporal cortex of monkeys as they learned to recognize new objects. They found that learning did not broadly rewire the visual system but instead made small shifts in how object information, including location, was represented, and computer models were able to predict these subtle changes.

This is one animal study, so it does not yet show how or whether this applies to human learning or perception in daily life.

July 15, 2026 · via MIT News (McGovern Institute for Brain Research)

Logical reasoning may not need the brain's language centers, MIT study finds

Researchers led by Evelina Fedorenko found that people with severe aphasia could still perform well on logical reasoning tasks and that brain scans showed the language network was not active during reasoning.

The study does not show which brain regions actually do the reasoning work or that language plays no role at all in thinking.

July 13, 2026 · via Tech Times

Replication Study Raises Questions About Dementia Prevention Estimates

A new analysis using data from the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study tested all 14 risk factors from a widely cited dementia prevention model at the same time. Only hearing loss, diabetes, and hypertension stayed statistically significant, which has led researchers to question how many dementia cases can truly be prevented worldwide.

This comes from one study population in Wisconsin, mostly white adults born in 1939, presented at a conference and not yet peer reviewed, so it does not prove the other 11 risk factors are unimportant or overturn the larger evidence base behind global prevention estimates.

July 13, 2026 · via ScienceDaily (Yale School of Medicine)

Yale Scientists Find Two Proteins That May Help Parkinson's Spread In The Brain

Yale researchers report that two proteins on neuron surfaces called mGluR4 and NPDC1 may help carry the toxic alpha synuclein protein between brain cells. In mice engineered to lack these proteins the animals kept more healthy dopamine cells and showed slower disease progression.

This is early stage mouse research using genetic changes rather than a drug, so it does not yet show that blocking these proteins is safe or effective for people.

July 12, 2026 · via ScienceDaily (University of Birmingham / University of Oxford)

Constipation drug shows early promise for depression related brain fog in small trial

In a small trial people with a history of depression who took the drug prucalopride for about a week did better on tests of memory, attention, and thinking speed than those who took a placebo. The drug acts on a serotonin receptor found in both the gut and the brain and no significant side effects were reported.

This was a small short term study so it does not prove the drug treats depression or brain fog in general or that it is safe over longer use.

July 12, 2026 · via NeurologyLive (Neurology journal)

Large Danish Study Finds Blood CGRP Levels Don't Reliably Signal Migraine

A cross-sectional study of over 1,700 people in Denmark's REFORM registry found that plasma CGRP levels did not clearly separate migraine patients from healthy controls. The researchers suggest that timing of blood draws and CGRP's short half-life may be part of why the blood test does not capture the migraine-related signaling it was meant to reflect.

This single snapshot study cannot tell us whether CGRP still matters during actual migraine attacks or in tracking treatment response over time.

July 11, 2026 · via ScienceDaily (Amsterdam UMC / Nature Communications)

A second pregnancy changes the brain differently than a first

A study that scanned 110 women repeatedly through pregnancy found that a second pregnancy creates its own pattern of brain changes rather than repeating what happened during a first pregnancy.

This is one observational study without long term follow up so it does not show what these brain changes mean for behavior or health.

July 11, 2026 · via The Transmitter

UCL Scientist Says AI Tools Can Now Mine Open Neuroscience Data For Quick Answers

A UCL neuroscientist argues that AI agents combined with large open datasets from groups like the Allen Institute and International Brain Lab can already answer specific factual questions, such as whether neurons in a brain area respond to visual stimuli, by computing directly from the data instead of searching published papers.

This is an opinion piece describing an emerging idea, not a study with tested accuracy rates or systematic evaluation of these AI agents.

July 11, 2026 · via Journal of Neuroscience

Astrocyte Receptor Loss Disrupts How Brain Synapses Form

A new study in mice found that losing a receptor called TrkB.T1 on astrocytes disrupts the formation of glutamate releasing synapses and normal contacts between astrocytes and neurons. The findings point to a role for this receptor in wiring the developing brain.

This is a single animal study and it does not show whether these changes affect behavior or learning or whether the same process happens in humans.

July 11, 2026 · via Journal of Neuroscience

A key nerve signal changes direction as young zebrafish grow up

Researchers studying zebrafish found that a common electrical signal in motor neurons called the M current works one way early in development and then flips to work the opposite way as the nervous system matures.

This finding comes from one study in zebrafish motor neurons so it does not show whether the same switch happens in other animals, other neuron types, or humans.

July 10, 2026 · via Journal of Neuroscience

Marmoset Brain Area Responds Selectively to Its Own Species Calls

Researchers recorded neural activity in a brain region called Area 32 in marmosets and found it responded selectively when the animals heard calls from other marmosets. The work adds new detail to ongoing research on how specific brain areas handle social vocal sounds.

This is one study and does not show that Area 32 is required for recognizing calls or that this selective response is unique to that region.

July 10, 2026 · via Federation of European Neuroscience Societies

Ear acupuncture worked no better than a fake version for migraine

In a randomized trial of 68 women with chronic migraine, ear acupuncture eased pain and its impact on daily life after 30 days, but a fake placebo version helped just as much, so the real technique did not beat placebo.

This is a small, preliminary conference result that is not yet peer reviewed, and because there was no untreated group the improvement could come from the placebo effect or from natural ups and downs in migraine.

July 9, 2026 · via The Transmitter

In mice, the brain updates feelings toward another animal without changing who it remembers

Memory neurons in the hippocampus held a steady record of another animal's identity while the strength of their links to the basolateral amygdala changed to update the emotional tag on that animal. This let a mouse switch from liking to disliking a familiar cagemate that had turned aggressive.

This was a single study in mice that used artificial techniques to switch neurons on and off, so it does not show that human feelings work the same way or that the circuit change by itself drives the behavior.

July 8, 2026 · via University of Rochester Medical Center

Gene therapy reached the whole mouse brain through the brain's own fluid system

Scientists engineered AAV viral vectors and injected them into the spinal fluid at the base of the brain, using the brain's glymphatic clearance system to carry the gene therapy throughout the brain while mostly sparing other organs. In mice carrying transplanted human glial cells, the vectors reached glial cells across the brain, which could point to a way past the barrier that normally keeps therapies out of the brain for diseases like multiple sclerosis and Huntington's.

This is an early delivery proof of concept in mice carrying human cells, so it shows the method can reach brain cells but does not show that it treats any disease or that it works in the far larger human brain.

July 8, 2026 · via The Transmitter

Different autism genes disrupt the same early brain development in mice

In 11 mouse models that each carried a different autism gene, the earliest brain changes looked strikingly alike, with neural stem cells maturing late and young neurons firing differently soon after birth. As the mice grew, their molecular signatures drifted apart, and by two weeks after birth about half of the gene activity changes were unique to each model.

This is one study in mice and no treatment was tested, so it does not show that targeting these early pathways would prevent or treat autism in people.

July 7, 2026 · via MIT News (McGovern Institute for Brain Research)

MIT scientists find a simple brain circuit that helps rodents judge distance with their whiskers

Researchers at MIT identified a straightforward brain circuit in rodents that turns touch signals from whisker contact into a sense of how far away an object is. The work points to a clear pathway in the brainstem that helps translate physical touch into a distance judgment.

This is a single rodent study and it does not show whether the same circuit works in other animals or in humans.

July 1, 2026 · via MIT News

Brain's Language Network May Be More Extensive Than Previously Thought

Using fMRI data from more than 700 people, MIT researchers found 17 brain regions outside the classic left hemisphere language areas, including parts of the cerebellum, hippocampus, and cerebral cortex, that also respond selectively to language, together making up about 5 percent of adult brain volume.

This is a single fMRI based study showing correlated brain activity, it does not prove these regions are actually needed for language or that the finding will hold up when tested in other ways.

June 25, 2026 · via University of Oslo via ScienceDaily

A new paper says Freud's old ideas about the mind echo today's prediction based theories of the brain

Researchers writing in the journal Entropy argue that Freud's century old ideas about how the mind works share notable similarities with the modern predictive processing theory of the brain, which holds that the brain constantly predicts what will happen next and updates itself based on new sensory information.

This is a theoretical comparison paper, not a new experiment, so it does not prove either framework correct or validate Freud's specific clinical claims.

June 22, 2026 · via ScienceDaily (Keck Medicine of USC)

Fish oil reaches the brain but does not improve memory, major trial finds

A two year placebo controlled trial in older adults at risk of Alzheimer's found that high dose fish oil supplements did reach the brain but did not meaningfully improve memory, cognition, or Alzheimer's related brain changes.

This single trial does not rule out possible benefits from different doses, longer follow up, or other subgroups, and independent replication is still needed.

June 1, 2026 · via UC San Francisco

Adaptive brain implant adjusts stimulation with each step for Parkinson's patients in small trial

In a study of 5 people with Parkinson's disease, a deep brain stimulator that reads step-related brain signals and adjusts its output within a fraction of a second improved gait symmetry and reduced falls compared with standard constant stimulation. Three participants completed the full randomized crossover comparison.

This was a small, short crossover trial in 5 people, so it does not show whether the walking and fall benefits hold up over months or years or in larger, more diverse groups.

January 1, 2026 · via Journal of Neuroscience

New Atlas Maps How Brain Surface Area Changes With Age

Researchers built a systems level atlas showing how cortical surface area shifts across the aging brain, published in the Journal of Neuroscience.

This is a single descriptive mapping study and it does not show what causes these changes or how they play out over time in individual people.

January 1, 2026 · via Journal of Neuroscience

Protein Protocadherin 9 Helps Specific Bipolar Cells Survive in the Developing Retina

In a study of developing mouse retinas, researchers found that the protein Protocadherin 9 promotes survival of specific types of bipolar cells. The work adds a piece to the puzzle of how visual circuits get wired correctly during development.

This is a single mouse study, so it does not yet show whether the same process happens in humans or how it affects long term vision.
