# The Neuroscience Review — Positioning & Voice Doc

*The north star for every post. When in doubt, come back here.*

---

## The One-Sentence Promise

**The Neuroscience Review explains real brain research with the rigor of a journal and the readability of a Sunday paper — so a smart non-scientist learns something true, and a science-literate reader still gets the depth they want.**

If a post doesn't deliver on *both* halves, it isn't ready to publish.

---

## Audience

Two readers sit on either shoulder when writing:

**Reader A — The Curious Generalist.** A smart adult who reads the NYT science section or *The Atlantic*. No formal bio background. Will bounce if a sentence assumes vocabulary they don't have. Rewards the writer who explains things clearly without talking down.

**Reader B — The Science-Literate Reader.** A premed, neuro undergrad, or working scientist. Will bounce if the post is fluff. Rewards depth, primary sources, mechanism-level explanation, and intellectual honesty about what's uncertain.

The discipline of this blog is **never losing either one in the same post.** Every claim should be readable for A and verifiable for B.

---

## Voice & Tone

- **Clean and journalistic.** Think Quanta Magazine. Not breezy, not stiff. Sentences earn their place.
- **Technically sound, never lax.** Accuracy is non-negotiable. If the science is uncertain, say so.
- **Confident, not condescending.** Explain like a knowledgeable friend, not a professor at a podium.
- **Sparing with metaphor.** A great analogy beats a paragraph of jargon. A bad analogy is worse than the jargon. Use them deliberately.
- **First person sparingly.** This isn't a personal blog. Use "I" only when your own reasoning or experience genuinely adds something.

**Avoid:** "fascinating," "amazing," "groundbreaking," "mind-blowing." Show the reader why it's interesting; don't tell them.

## Format

Two post types, each with a clear job:

### Paper Breakdowns (60% of posts)
Take one recent neuroscience paper and unpack it. Structure:
1. **The hook** — one question or claim the paper answers
2. **Why this matters** — the bigger picture in 2–4 sentences
3. **What they did** — methods in plain language
4. **What they found** — the result, no spin
5. **What it doesn't mean** — caveats, limits, what's still unknown
6. **Why you should care** — the takeaway, honestly

Target length: **1,500–2,500 words.** Always link the original paper. Always include a one-line summary at the top for skimmers.

### First-Principles Deep Dives (40% of posts)
Build a concept from scratch. "What is a neuron, really?" "How does memory actually work?" These are the evergreen posts — Google will send people to them for years.

Target length: **2,000–3,500 words.** Use diagrams. Define every term the first time it appears. Link to your other posts for related concepts so the blog cross-references itself.

## Editorial Standards (Non-Negotiables)

1. **Every factual claim is sourced or sourceable.** Link to primary literature where possible; review articles only if necessary.
2. **No hedging without explaining.** "Studies suggest" is lazy. Say *which* studies, in *what* population, with *what* effect size.
3. **Don't conflate animal models with humans.** A mouse study is a mouse study. Say so.
4. **Distinguish correlation from causation.** Every. Single. Time.
5. **When something is contested, present both sides** — don't quietly side with the prettier story.
6. **Acknowledge what you don't know.** "Here's what I couldn't figure out" is the most credible sentence in science writing.

## Differentiation — Why This Blog Exists

Most neuroscience writing falls into one of two buckets:

- **Pop-sci** (Big Think, viral Twitter threads): Readable but loose. Sensationalizes, oversimplifies, gets the science slightly wrong.
- **Academic** (papers, reviews, textbooks): Accurate but impenetrable to anyone outside the field.

**The Neuroscience Review lives in the gap.** Accuracy that doesn't compromise readability. Readability that doesn't compromise accuracy. The blog you'd give your smart aunt *and* recommend to a med student.

## First 10 Post Ideas

A mix of paper breakdowns (PB) and first-principles (FP) to seed the blog:

1. **(FP)** What is a neuron, really? — The foundational post. Builds vocabulary the rest of the blog will use.
2. **(FP)** How does memory actually work? — Long-term vs. short-term, hippocampus, consolidation, the still-unknown bits.
3. **(PB)** The latest on psychedelics and depression — pick a 2025–2026 trial paper, unpack it honestly.
4. **(FP)** The blood-brain barrier — what gets in, what doesn't, why this matters for every drug ever invented.
5. **(PB)** A recent Alzheimer's paper — there's a new one every month; pick the most interesting and explain why the field is or isn't excited.
6. **(FP)** Why sleep matters for the brain — glymphatic system, memory consolidation, what happens when you skip it.
7. **(PB)** Something from the Columbia Mass Murder Database / Girgis lab — you have insider context; use it.
8. **(FP)** Dopamine doesn't do what you think it does — corrects the "dopamine = pleasure" pop-sci myth.
9. **(PB)** A recent paper on neuroplasticity — bonus credibility if it pushes back against a popular claim.
10. **(FP)** What an fMRI actually measures — because nobody who cites fMRI studies in articles knows.

## Publishing Cadence

- **Goal: 1 post / week** for the first 10–20 posts.
- **Minimum viable cadence: 1 post / 2 weeks.** Lower than that and you lose momentum + algorithmic favor.
- **Reassess at post 20.** If you're still writing and learning, keep going. If it feels forced, pivot or pause.

---

## Success Metrics (90 days)

- **20 published posts**
- **500 email subscribers** (achievable with consistency + 1–2 posts shared in the right neuro subreddits)
- **One post breaks 5,000 views** (signal that your voice is finding an audience)
- **At least one Substack Recommendation** from a larger neuro/science writer

These are stretch goals, not pass/fail. The real win in 90 days is having a body of work you're proud of.

## Reminders to Future-You

- The point of "Made Easy" is not "made shallow." It's "made navigable."
- If you can't explain it without jargon, you don't understand it yet.
- The best science writing makes the reader feel smarter, not the writer.
- Consistency beats brilliance. A good post every week for a year > one perfect post.
